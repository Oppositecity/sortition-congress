Not hypothetical

Athens · 5th c. BC

The kleroterion

A stone slab that randomised jurors and council members daily. Athenians treated election as the oligarchic method and the lot as the democratic one — the reverse of the modern instinct.

Venice · 1268–1797

Ten rounds of chance

The Doge was chosen by an alternating sequence of lot and vote, repeated ten times. The randomness was there to make the process too expensive to rig.

Ireland · 2016–18

The Citizens' Assembly

Ninety-nine randomly selected citizens deliberated on abortion and recommended repealing the Eighth Amendment. The referendum that followed matched them closely.

France · 2019–20

Convention Citoyenne

A hundred and fifty people drawn by lot spent nine months on climate policy and returned 149 proposals — and a long argument about what governments owe an assembly they convened.

Belgium · 2019–

A permanent council

The German-speaking Community seats an ongoing citizens' council chosen by lot alongside its elected parliament. Not a one-off panel — a standing institution.

Everywhere · now

The jury

Sortition already decides whether you go to prison. We accept random selection for the gravest decisions a state makes, and consider it unthinkable for the mildest.

The case against

Competence. Legislating is a skill. A random plumber has no experience of budget reconciliation, and the lottery offers no way to remove someone who turns out to be bad at it.

Accountability. An elected member can be thrown out. A member drawn by lot answers to no one, and a term that ends automatically is not a term anyone can shorten.

Capture. Amateurs with no staff and no institutional memory are easier for lobbyists and civil servants to steer — the expertise merely relocates to people nobody chose at all.

Consent. Most people do not want the job. A chamber of conscripts is representative in composition and unrepresentative in enthusiasm, and self-selection out of the pool reintroduces the bias sortition was meant to remove.

Most serious proposals answer these by pairing a sortition chamber with an elected one, or by using lot for review and agenda-setting rather than for drafting law.

Method

People, not percentages. Each seat is sampled as a whole person: age first, then schooling conditioned on age, work conditioned on both, and income conditioned on all three. Sampling each attribute independently — the usual shortcut — produces twenty-two-year-old retirees with graduate degrees. The correlations are approximate, but they are there.

Population figures are rounded U.S. adult shares. Elected figures are approximate for the current Congress and should be treated as indicative, not authoritative.

One number is a definition, not a finding. Every member of Congress earns a salary well above $100,000, so the elected chamber is 100% high-income by construction. That row measures the pay of the office, not the wealth someone brought to it. The honest version of the income comparison is the one this instrument cannot show: net worth before taking office.

Race and sex are sampled close to population shares, with a mild age correlation. They are reported because the elected chamber's gaps are the most documented, not because they are the deepest.
